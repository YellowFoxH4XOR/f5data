Description

A recently published forensic investigation by Sygnia described a threat actor using an F5 BIG-IP to obtain persistence and aid the exfiltration of information. This is F5s response to that investigation.

Environment

All BIG-IP products

Cause

None

Recommended Actions

To protect systems from exploitation F5 recommends customers:

  • Run the most current version to optimize the security and performance of their systems
  • Do not expose the control plane network to untrusted sources including the Internet
  • Utilize the F5 iHealth Diagnostic Tool to verify the proper operation of their system and ensure it is functioning at peak efficiency

Additional Information

As the third-party investigation notes, "Both F5 appliances were running an outdated, vulnerable, operating system. The threat actor may have leveraged one of the vulnerabilities to gain remote access to the appliances."
 
F5 publishes Quarterly Security Notifications and Additional Security Announcements (see K12201527) in an effort to keep customers informed of any F5 vulnerabilities which may impact their systems and provide remediation and update steps to secure them.
 
F5 does not have any additional information beyond the public analysis and is not aware of any zero-day or unknown vulnerabilities being exploited by this threat actor. Based on the public information available it seems most likely that the devices were compromised because their management interfaces were exposed to the Internet (which is contrary to F5 advice and best practices) and they were running an old version of BIG-IP software impacted by a disclosed vulnerability for which a fixed version is available.
 
F5 recommends customers review K13092: Overview of securing access to the BIG-IP system for guidance on securing access to the management interface and to ensure you are running an up to date version.
 
The third-party investigation lists a number of Indicators of Compromise (IOCs) which F5 has not been able to verify, however concerned customers may wish to check their systems for the presence of:
  • Modifications to /etc/rc.local to run unknown binaries (/shared/tmp/pmcd and /shared/tmp/mcdp per the third-party article)
  • The presence of the following binaries in any location:
    • pmcd
    • mcdp
    • samrid
    • esrde
The third-party investigation lists SHA1 hashes of these binaries which F5 has not verified.
 
Note: mcpd is a legitimate F5 binary located in /bin/mcpd and should not be confused with 'mcdp' as noted in the third-party investigation.
 
F5s iHealth diagnostic service has heuristics which alert for common issues such as:
  • Access to the management interface (the Configuration Utility or SSH) from Internet hosts
  • Weak password configuration
  • Unknown binaries running on the system
  • Suspicious modifications to system files

Additionally, the sys-icheck utility will warn of modification to certain files (including /etc/rc.local), instructions for running this can be found in K9118: Overview of the sys-icheck utility. File modification alone is not evidence of compromise, an administrator must still manually inspect the file to ascertain if the modification is malicious or not.

If you have any concerns and suspect compromise, F5 suggests customers review K11438344: Considerations and guidance when you suspect a security compromise on a BIG-IP system.

 
In summary, F5 appreciates the opportunity to work with third parties to identify and resolve issues on behalf of our customers. As organizations work to stay on top of the growing complexity of their infrastructure and the increasing sophistication of cyber threats, we recommend customers 1) run the most current version to optimize the security and performance of their systems, 2) do not expose the control plane network to untrusted sources including the Internet, and 3) utilize the F5 iHealth Diagnostic Tool to verify the proper operation of their system and ensure it is functioning at peak efficiency.
