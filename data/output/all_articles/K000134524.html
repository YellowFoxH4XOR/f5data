Description

Since CISA published Alert AA23-075A regarding LockBit 3.0 ("LockBit Black") there has been renewed interest in whether F5 products a vulnerable to LockBit 3.0, and how F5 products can protect against it.

Environment

  • F5 Products deployed in customer infrastructure
  • Potentially vulnerable Windows target machines (e.g., client PCs)

Cause

The CISA alert in question (AA23-075A) details LockBit 3.0 ("LockBit Black") as Windows malware, and the alert lists the potential delivery mechanisms as:
Affiliates deploying LockBit 3.0 ransomware gain initial access to victim networks via remote desktop protocol (RDP) exploitation [T1133], drive-by compromise [T1189], phishing campaigns [T1566], abuse of valid accounts [T1078], and exploitation of public-facing applications [T1190].

Recommended Actions

F5 products can be deployed to assist in protecting against the exploitation of public-facing applications, for example:

  • BIG-IP Advanced WAF, NGINX App Protect and F5 Distributed Cloud can be used to protect against exploitation of both known and unknown (0day) vulnerabilities in public-facing web applications.

  • BIG-IP AFM, NGINX and F5 Distributed Cloud can protect against Denial of Service attacks against infrastructure which could be used to mask other malicious activities

Additional Information

As noted above, this variant of LockBit 3.0 is Windows malware and, therefore, F5 products are not vulnerable as they are not Windows-based products.

Note: There are variants of LockBit which target Linux (and/or ESXi) hosts sometimes named "LockBit Linux-ESXi". Linux-based products from F5 (such as BIG-IP) may be, or become, targets of LockBit Linux-ESXi or other Linux-based malware. Linux systems are not "vulnerable" to malware in the sense of a CVE; malware is simply a class of software which, in this case, could be run on a Linux system. As CISA notes, the delivery mechanism for malware is separate and requires the attacker to exploit a known vulnerability on the target system, to execute a phishing attack against an administrator of that system, to be able to directly gain access to a system exposed to the Internet using guessed or stolen credentials, or to have physical access to the system.

Securing your systems against malware, therefore, means securing administrative access to systems and ensuring your employees and administrators follow security best practices at all times.
