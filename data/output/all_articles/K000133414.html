Topic

Creating and maintaining a secure SSL/TLS deployment can be time-consuming. First, you should assess the needs of your application, while finding a balance of security, usability and performance; understanding the objectives of your application will help you select the appropriate cryptographic algorithms and key management strategies. For example, you should know if the primary objective of your application is to store data securely, transmit and receive data, accept online payments or a combination. In addition, the security requirements for your application may vary depending on the expected client traffic. Refer to the following application descriptions for reference:

  • High-level security: Modern application with clients that support TLS 1.3 and don't need backward compatibility.
  • Mid-level security: General-purpose application with a wide variety of clients.
  • Low-level security: Legacy application with some older clients that don't support modern protocol versions.

Next, you must prepare and configure your network infrastructure. For the BIG-IP administrators, this usually involves tasks such as, choosing a reputable Certificate Authority (CA), deciding on the appropriate SSL/TLS certificate, determining how to manage and secure your private keys, configuring BIG-IP local traffic objects, such as SSL/TLS profiles and virtual servers, and testing and deploying your application. Once the application is deployed, it is important to monitor and update your SSL/TLS deployment as vulnerabilities are discovered and the threat landscape changes for your organization.

To help you navigate this process, this document provides security-minded recommendations and important information to consider when creating and maintaining secure SSL/TLS deployments.

Certificate Authorities (CAs)

The CA is the entity that signs and issues your digital certificates in compliance with a Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) policy. Make sure to research prospective CAs and choose a reputable CA whose core business is PKI; doing so gives you the assurance that your certificate lifecycle management is in the hands of security experts. When researching prospective CAs, review the following recommendations:

RecommendationDetailsResource
Choose a CA whose core business is Public Key Infrastructure (PKI).Public key infrastructure (PKI) outlines the policies and procedures needed to establish and manage public key encryption. Generally, CAs whose core business is PKI have the expertise to handle problems in the event that something goes wrong with your certificate deployment.
  • PKI Consortium
Choose a CA that offers products, services and diagnostic tools required by your organization.CAs should provide current products, services and diagnostic tools that are relevant in the industry and required by your organization. For example, choose a CA that offers certificates with the features required by your site and diagnostic tools for checking your SSL/TLS deployment.
  • SSL Insights (Types of SSL Certificates)
Verify whether the CA offers certificate lifecycle management tools.Many CAs provide tools to facilitate certificate lifecycle management. For example, a CA may provide APIs to automate certificate-related operations.
  • TLS Server Certificate Management (NIST SP 1800-16)
Verify whether the CA participates in industry-related organizations.Industry-related organizations are those that are committed to improving policies, procedures, best practices and standards related to Public Key Infrastructure (PKI).
  • CA/Browser Forum
  • PKI Consortium
Limit the number of CAs you use to issue certificates for your organization.The root certificate of each CA that you use to issue certificates for your organization must be trusted by the clients connecting to your application. Using certificates from a large number of CAs makes maintaining trusted CA certificates on clients burdensome.
  • TLS Server Certificate Management (NIST SP 1800-16)
Verify that the CA has favorable reviews.CA reviews may cover topics such as customer support, ease of managing SSL certificates and overall customer satisfaction.
  • SSL Shopper (CA reviews)

Certificates and private keys

The core of a secure SSL/TLS deployment consists of the public and private key pair which provides authentication and protects the confidentiality and integrity of the data. The certificate contains the public key which is intended for public distribution, while the private key should be stored securely by your organization.

Certificate security depends on a number of factors such as, the strength of the private key that was used to sign the certificate, the strength of the hashing function used in the signature, and security precautions taken for private key. When planning and maintaining your SSL/TLS certificate deployment, refer to the following information:

RecommendationDetailsResource
Implement a certificate and key management plan.Determine the certificate and key management requirements for your organization and include all processes related to storing and maintaining certificates and private keys. Certificate and key management planning involves recommendations such as the following:
  • Create a comprehensive inventory of your SSL/TLS certificates and keys
  • Manage certificate / key life cycle
  • Understand key storage options for your organization
  • Make plans for compromised keys
  • TLS Server Certificate Management (NIST SP 1800-16)
  • OWASP Key Management Cheat Sheet
  • NIST Recommendation for Key Management
Decide on the appropriate public key algorithm for your usage context and data.RSA and ECDSA are the most common asymmetric public key algorithms (also referred to as authentication algorithms) used to sign certificates and verify the identity of the server (and optionally, client), during the SSL/TLS handshake. Review the following information as well as the content in the Resources column when deciding on the appropriate algorithm:
  • RSA
    • RSA is a more widely adopted and mature algorithm.
    • Some legacy systems may only support RSA.
    • RSA requires larger key sizes to provide the same security level as ECDSA.
  • ECDSA
    • Most modern clients support ECDSA.
    • Preferred for mobile and IoT applications.
    • ECDSA allows for smaller key sizes to provide the same level of security as RSA, increasing performance and scalability.
  • NIST Digital Signature Standard (FIPS 186-5)
  • K15194: Overview of BIG-IP SSL/TLS cipher suites
  • Comparing ECDSA vs RSA
Use strong private keys.Key size recommendations vary depending on what algorithm you are using and for what purpose. In general, smaller key sizes require less bandwidth to set up an SSL/TLS connection, while larger key sizes provide better security and slower performance. At a minimum, the following key sizes are recommended to achieve good security while maintaining performance:
  • RSA key: 2048-bit
  • ECDSA key: 256-bit
  • NIST Transitioning the Use of Cryptographic Algorithms and Key Lengths (SP 800-131A)
  • OWASP Key Management Cheat Sheet
Implement protection and recovery strategies for private keys.
  • For BIG-IP systems, limit access to private keys and only grant Administrator or Certificate Manager user roles to trusted users.
  • Generate and maintain private keys only on a trusted devices, such as HSMs or F5 systems.
  • When renewing a certificate make sure to generate a new private key.
  • Revoke any certificate that is associated with a compromised key (for example, using OCSP stapling).
  • Monitor SSL certificate and intermediate CA certificate expiration and ensure that your certificates are renewed prior to the expiration date.
  • NIST Recommendation for Key Management
  • Manual : BIG-IP Systems: User Account Administration
  • K75106155: Configuring OCSP stapling (13.x - 16.x)
  • K14318: Monitoring SSL certificate expiration on the BIG-IP system
  • K000135447: Generate a new private key when renewing an SSL/TLS certificate
Use a SSL/TLS certificate signed by a CA.SSL/TLS certificates can be self-signed or signed by a certificate authority. A self-signed certificate is signed by its own private key and is less secure. A CA certificate is signed by the CA's private key which establishes a chain of trust to the root. CA certificates are recommended for production use.
  • K41280190: Understanding the SSL Certificate Chain
  • NIST Recommendation for Key Management
Choose a certificate type that offers the appropriate level of data protection for your site.When purchasing a certificate, review the common certificate types and choose a certificate type that offers the appropriate level of data protection for your site. For production sites, such as enterprise, financial or e-commerce sites, make sure your certificates are issued by a reputable Certificate Authority (CA) and compare certificate prices and data protection levels to find the right certificate for your needs. SSL certificates are generally categorized as follows:
  • Domain Validation (DV)
    • A DV certificate only requires you to verify that you are the owner of your domain. Therefore, DV certificates are less trustworthy than other certificate types.
    • Not recommended for sites that deal with sensitive data or financial transactions.
  • Organizational Validation (OV)
    • For OV certificates the issuing CA validates your organizational information to make sure you are an actual organization. OV certificates are more trustworthy to a consumer because they are assured that they are doing business with a real corporate entity.
    • Appropriate for sites that collect sensitive data, such as login credentials and personal details.
  • Extended Validation (EV)
    • EV certificates align with the PCI-DSS, and HIPPA standards and are recognized as the most trustworthy type of SSL certificate. The issuing CA takes rigorous steps to verify that your business is legitimate and in good standing.
    • Appropriate for sites that require the most advanced level of data protection, such as accepting online payments or transmitting highly sensitive data.
  • SSL Insights (Types of SSL Certificates)
  • SSL Shopper (Certificate Wizard)
Make sure your certificates are signed with an approved signature algorithm and key length and a strong hash algorithm.CAs sign certificates so their authenticity can be verified. Certificate signatures are generated using digital signature algorithms (for example, RSA or ECDSA) and hash algorithms (for example, SHA256). The signature legitimizes the certificate by providing cryptographic proof that your CA issued the certificate and it was not altered by a 3rd party. The SHA256 hashing algorithm (based on SHA-2) is the current internet cryptographic standard and adopted by major CAs.
  • Transitioning the Use of Cryptographic Algorithms and Key Lengths (NIST SP 800-131A)
  • NIST Policy on Hash Functions
  • NIST Secure Hash Standard (FIPS 180-4)
Consider the certificate hostname coverage for your site and whether you will need a SSL Subject Alternative Name (SAN) certificate.Make sure that your SSL/TLS certificates cover all names and IP addresses you plan to use with your site (for example, with and without www). Doing so avoids invalid certificate warnings. SAN certificates or Unified Communication (UCC) certificates allows you to specify multiple alternative domains for your certificate.
  • K13471: Creating SSL SAN certificates and CSRs using the Configuration utility or tmsh
Use CAA DNS records to protect your domain against mis-issued SSL/TLS certificates.CAA DNS resource records allow DNS domain name holders to specify one or more CAs to issue certificates for their domain names; CAs that are not specified in the CAA resource records are not permitted to issue certificates for the domain name.
  • K32655308: Protect your domain against mis-issued SSL/TLS certificates using CAA DNS records

Certificate signing request (CSR)

A CSR is an encoded file that you provide to your CA when applying for a digital certificate. In most cases the CSR is generated on the device in which the certificate will be installed and contains your public key and other information that identifies your company and domain name. The Certificate Authority (CA) uses this information to create your digital certificate. Before creating a CSR, review the following recommendations:

RecommendationDetailsResource
Verify the specific information needed to generate the CSR.The CSR contains information about your company that is embedded in the certificate, such as the Common Name, Organization and Country.Consult with your organization and CA to determine the required information.
Generate the CSR (and resulting private key) on the F5 device in which the certificate will be installed.When you generate the CSR on the F5 device, you ensure that the resulting private key remains on the local device and does not need to be copied to a remote system.
  • K14620: Manage SSL certificates for BIG-IP systems using the Configuration utility
When renewing SSL certificates, generate a new CSR and private key.Renewing a certificate using a new private key is recommended as it lessens the chance of unauthorized use in the event your key was compromised.
  • K000135447: Generate a new private key when renewing an SSL/TLS certificate

SSL/TLS protocols and ciphers

The BIG-IP system supports TLS protocols and a large set of cipher suites that you can choose from to build the SSL cipher string used for security negotiation. F5 recommends using current SSL/TLS protocols (TLS 1.2 or 1.3). In addition, when building a cipher string you should use the BIG-IP cipher rules and groups configuration objects rather than manually configuring a cipher string; manually configuring a raw cipher string can result in typos and be unsecure. Before creating SSL cipher strings on the BIG-IP system, refer to the following recommendations:

RecommendationDetailsResource
Identify the security requirements for your site and the types of clients that will access your application.Identifying the types of clients that will access your application will help you build the SSL cipher string used for security negotiation. Refer to the following application descriptions for reference:
  • High-level security: Modern application with no requirement to support older clients.
  • Mid-level security: General-purpose application with a wide variety of clients.
  • Low-level security: Legacy application with some older clients that don't support modern protocol versions.
In addition, a configuration generator may help you identify the security requirements for your site. For example, the Mozilla Foundation provides a configuration generator for web, database, and mail software. This kind of tool can help you decide which TLS versions and cipher suites to enable for your application.
  • Mozilla SSL Configuration Generator
Review the SSL/TLS cipher suites available on the BIG-IP system.A cipher suite is a combination of algorithms used to negotiate the security settings for SSL/TLS connections. Review the content in the Resources column for information about SSL/TLS cipher terminology and cipher combinations available on the BIG-IP system.
  • K15194: Overview of BIG-IP SSL/TLS cipher suites
  • K000134647: SSL ciphers used in the default SSL profiles (17.x)
Use cipher suites that support perfect forward secrecy (PFS) when possible.PFS assures that a compromised private key will not also compromise the privacy of past sessions. When possible, prefer ephemeral keys over static keys (for example, use DHE and ECDHE). On the BIG-IP system, to use cipher suites that utilize ECDHE key exchange exclusively you can configure the SSL profile to use the f5-ecc cipher group.
  • NIST Guidelines for the Selection, Configuration, and Use of Transport Layer Security (TLS) Implementations (SP 800-52 Rev2)
  • K15194: Overview of BIG-IP SSL/TLS cipher suites
For high-level security applications, refer to the following general recommendations:
  • Protocols
    • Enable TLS 1.3
    • Disable prior TLS versions as required
  • Ciphers
    • Create a cipher group and add the f5-secure and f5-default cipher rules to the group.
    • Create a cipher rule with any ciphers that you want to exclude and add the rule to the cipher group's Exclude list.
Examples of high-level security applications are those that accept online payments or process sensitive data. These types of applications may only accept clients that support the most current versions of TLS, such as TLS 1.3, and may not require backward compatibility. For high-level security applications you may need to enable TLS 1.3 and optionally disable earlier TLS versions in the SSL profile. In addition, you can use a cipher group to specify which cipher suites will be allowed when the BIG-IP system negotiates new SSL/TLS connections. You can also use cipher rules to further restrict sets of cipher suites.

Note: Per NIST recommendations, servers that support TLS 1.3 should be configured to use TLS 1.2 as well. However, TLS 1.2 may be disabled on servers that support TLS 1.3 if it has been determined that TLS 1.2 is not needed for interoperability.
  • K10251520: BIG-IP support for TLS 1.3
  • K33000012: How to disable SSL protocols or specific versions of TLS on an SSL profile such as TLS 1.0 and TLS 1.1
  • K10866411: Creating a custom cipher group using the Configuration utility
For mid-level security applications, refer to the following general recommendations:
  • Protocols
    • Enable TLS 1.3 if required
    • Disable prior TLS versions as required
  • Ciphers
    • Create a cipher group and add the f5-default cipher rule to the group.
    • Create a cipher rule with any ciphers that you want to exclude and add the rule to the cipher group's Exclude list.
Examples of mid-level security applications are those that require high security but are not mission-critical applications. These types of applications may support a wide variety of clients. For mid-level security applications you may need to enable or disable TLS versions in the SSL profile as required by your application. In addition, you can use a cipher group to specify which cipher suites will be allowed when the BIG-IP system negotiates new SSL connections. You can also use cipher rules to further restrict sets of cipher suites.
  • K10251520: BIG-IP support for TLS 1.3
  • K33000012: How to disable SSL protocols or specific versions of TLS on an SSL profile such as TLS 1.0 and TLS 1.1
  • K10866411: Creating a custom cipher group using the Configuration utility
For low-level security applications, refer to the following general recommendations:
  • Ciphers
    • Create a cipher group and add the f5-default cipher rule to the group.
    • Create a cipher rule with any additional ciphers that you want to add and add the rule to the cipher group's Allow list.
Examples of low-level security applications are those that do not require the highest security. These types of applications may support older clients. For low-level security applications you can use a cipher group to specify which cipher suites will be allowed when the BIG-IP system negotiates new SSL connections. You can also use cipher rules to allow additional sets of cipher suites that are not included in the default cipher group.
  • K10866411: Creating a custom cipher group using the Configuration utility
  • K65292843: Creating a custom cipher group using the tmsh utility
If you plan to manually configure a raw cipher string in the SSL profile, append cipher suites to the DEFAULT cipher string.F5 recommends using the cipher rules and groups configuration objects rather than manually configuring a cipher string; manually configuring a raw cipher string can result in typos and be unsecure. However, if you do manually configure a cipher string, always append cipher suites to the DEFAULT cipher string. For example, the following string allows the default ciphers for the SSL profile and blocks the AES128-SHA cipher:
  • DEFAULT:!AES128-SHA
  • K01770517: Configuring the cipher strength for SSL profiles (14.x - 17.x)
